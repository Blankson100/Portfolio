Crop Care

Introduction

Crop Care is an AI-powered personal assistant for farmers. Point your phone at a diseased leaf, and it identifies the problem, suggests treatment, and walks you through treatment, in your own language.

No agricultural training needed and no internet connection needed either. It's built for the moment it actually matters: standing in a field, looking at a plant that wasn't there yesterday, needing an answer now instead of in three days.

Whether it's a single potted plant at home or a working farm, the same care applies. Crop Care scales with whoever's using it.

The Problem

Plant diseases destroy 20 to 40 percent of global crop yields every year, costing the world economy over $220 billion. Most of that's preventable if caught early, but for most farmers, expert diagnosis is slow to reach them, if it reaches them at all. In many regions, one extension officer covers several thousand farmers, so by the time help arrives, the disease has already spread.

Existing tools don't close that gap. They're often built around a narrow set of crops and diseases, tested in conditions far removed from an actual field, and rarely account for how a farmer describes what they're seeing, in their own words, their own language.

What's missing is a system that becomes a personal assistant to farmers, one that understands them and translates for them based on where they are and what language they speak, so the answer they get back actually makes sense to them.

The App

See how it works
Plant AI: Crop Care uses computer vision to identify diseases across all plant types, and language AI to deliver treatment in local languages. The app connects farmers to nearby supplies through an accessibility-first interface.

Design Process

Research started with FAO and World Bank data to understand the scale of the problem, then a competitive audit across six existing apps: PlantNet, PlantVillage Nuru, iNaturalist, Google Lens, Agrio, and Plantix. None of them met more than half the criteria that actually mattered, language support, treatment guidance, offline use, simplicity.

The camera screen went through three versions. The first had no guidance at all, and testers didn't know how close to hold the phone or how to frame the leaf. The second added instructions, but the text blocked the viewfinder. The final version cut it down to two short prompts that sit above the camera without getting in the way.

The result screen changed the most. Early versions dumped everything, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, into one long scroll, and testers missed half of it. Splitting it into clear sections fixed that: a user can read the diagnosis in under three seconds, then choose how deep to go.

Visually, the whole app leans on deep greens pulled from healthy crop leaves, because it should look like what it's for. White backgrounds keep the result and history screens legible outdoors. Icons always carry a text label, since not every user is used to reading icons alone.
